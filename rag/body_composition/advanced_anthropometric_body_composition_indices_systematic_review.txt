Abstract  
Anthropometric assessment is widely used in sports science, nutrition, and health evaluation as a 
practical and non-invasive method for analyzing body composition, physical performance, and 
metabolic health. This review aimed to synthesize current evidence regarding the role of anthropometric 
parameters in sports sciences, nutritional assessment, and health monitoring. 
A comprehensive literature search was conducted across major scientific databases, covering 
publications from 2020 to 2026. The findings indicate that anthropometric variables such as body mass 
index (BMI), waist circumference, waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), skinfold thickness, body 
circumferences, fat mass percentage (FM%), fat-free mass index (FFMI), and skeletal muscle mass 
indicators are strongly associated with athletic performance, nutritional status, and cardiometabolic risk. 
In sports sciences, these measurements are widely applied for talent identification, training monitoring, 
and performance optimization. In clinical and nutritional contexts, they contribute to the assessment of 
obesity, sarcopenia, malnutrition, and metabolic disorders. Advanced indices such as Body Roundness 
Index (BRI), A Body Shape Index (ABSI), somatotype analysis, and Phantom Z-scores provide 
improved evaluation of body proportionality and fat distribution. 
Despite their broad applicability, anthropometric methods present limitations related to measurement 
variability and population specificity. Therefore, combining anthropometric assessment with advanced 
body composition techniques is recommended to improve accuracy and individualized evaluation.

The future of anthropometric science is expected to evolve toward AI-driven digital assessment 
systems integrated with personalized medicine and population-specific predictive models, 
allowing highly accurate, real-time, and individualized evaluation of body composition,

Introduction 
In the field of sport sciences and nutritional sciences, anthropometric profiling plays a central role in 
estimating physical characteristics connected with athletic performances, training adaptation, and body 
composition optimization 1,2. In this context, previous studies have reported that the changes in 
anthropometric variables are strictly associated with physical fitness, strength development, and sport-
specific performance, support their importance in talent identification and athlete monitoring 3,4. These 
anthropometric measurements, including body mass index (BMI), skinfold thickness, and body 
circumference are widely used as non-invasive, cost-effective tools for assessing growth, development, 
and body composition across different populations in anthropometric characterization 5,6. 
Otherwise, the evaluation of nutritional status which is a key determinant of health and physical 
capacity is closely related to anthropometric measurements. Nutritional assessment using body 
composition indices such as BMI-for-age, weight-for-height, and fat percentage provides insight into 
undernutrition, overnutrition, and related health risks such as obesity and related cardiovascular diseases 
7,8. In adolescent and young adult populations, several studies have reported significant associations 
between nutritional status, dietary intake, and physical fitness levels, emphasizing the integrative role 
of nutrition in health and development 3,9.   
Additionally, in health assessment works, Human body metrics are usually used to monitor 
cardiovascular disease risk, metabolic status, and physical ability. They are frequently combined with 
biochemical and physiological signs to provide a comprehensive and exhaustive evaluation of physical 
health status in both clinical and athletic populations 10,11. For instance, standardized preparticipation 
evaluations in athletes incorporate Body size parameters and fitness assessments to ensure safe 
participation and to detect potential health risks 10. 
Recent line of evidence also underscores the interrelationship between morphometric characteristics, 
physical activity levels, and health outcomes. In adolescents, significant associations have been 
observed between body composition, physical activity, and physical fitness, suggesting that 
anthropometric indices can serve as predictive indicators of overall health and functional capacity 12 10. 
Similarly, in older adults, body composition assessments are used to monitor health decline, frailty, and 
nutritional deterioration over time 8,13. 
Nevertheless, despite their widespread use, Body size parameters present some limitations, principally 
when used separately. For example, BMI alone does not differentiate between fat mass and lean mass, 
which may lead to misclassification of nutritional status and health risks 14. Consequently, several 
current research advocate for the combined use of anthropometry with other assessment tools, such as 
bioelectrical impedance analysis and physical performance tests, to improve the accuracy of health and 
nutritional evaluations 15,16.

In light of these considerations, a multidisciplinary approach integrating anthropometric parameters, 
physical fitness, nutritional assessment, and health indicators is indispensable to better comprehend 
human performance, improving health outcomes and preventive care strategies. Thus, the purpose 
of the present review is to synthesize current knowledge on the used anthropometric parameters using 
in sport sciences, focusing on their role in nutritional status evaluation and physical health assessment. 
A structured literature search was conducted to identify and critically evaluate recent studies related to 
anthropometric parameters, body composition indices, and their applications in sports performance, 
nutrition, and health assessment. 
Materials and methods 
Review Design and Research Question 
This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for 
Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines. The review aimed to 
identify, synthesize, and critically evaluate the current evidence regarding anthropometric 
parameters and body composition indicators used in sports sciences, nutrition, and health 
assessment. 
The review was guided by the following PICO-based research question: 
Population (P): Athletes, physically active individuals, healthy adults, adolescents, and 
clinical populations. 
Intervention/Exposure (I): Assessment using anthropometric parameters and body 
composition indicators. 
Comparator (C): Traditional anthropometric measures (e.g., body mass index, body weight, 
waist circumference) or alternative body composition assessment methods. 
Outcomes (O): Evaluation of body composition, nutritional status, physical performance, 
metabolic health, cardiometabolic risk, and health-related outcomes. 
The primary research question was: 
"What anthropometric parameters and body composition indicators are currently used and 
validated for assessing body composition, athletic performance, nutritional status, and health 
outcomes in sports and nutrition sciences?"

Information Sources and Literature Search Strategy 
The Web of Science, PubMed, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink, MDPI, Wiley Online Library, and Taylor 
& Francis Online, and Scopus databases were searched to identify studies published between January 
2020 and April 2026, and aimed to identify, analyze, discuss or measure anthropometric parameters, 
body size and physical performances.  
The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms related 
to anthropometry, body composition, sports sciences, nutrition, and health assessment. The 
following search equation was used and adapted according to each database: 
("anthropometric parameters" OR anthropometry OR "body composition" OR BMI OR "body 
mass index" OR WHR OR WHtR OR ABSI OR BRI OR VAI OR CI OR FFMI OR FFM OR 
FM% OR somatotype OR "body adiposity index" OR "fat mass" OR "fat-free mass" OR 
"skeletal muscle mass") AND ("sports science" OR athletes OR "physical performance" OR 
exercise OR nutrition OR health OR "health assessment" OR "metabolic health" OR 
"cardiometabolic risk") 
In addition, the reference lists of relevant articles were manually screened to identify 
potentially eligible studies not retrieved through electronic database searches.

Study Selection Process 
The study selection process was performed according to PRISMA 2020 recommendations. 
All retrieved references were exported to Zotero reference management software. Duplicate 
records were identified and removed using both automated and manual procedures. 
The screening process consisted of two sequential phases: 
Title and Abstract Screening: Titles and abstracts were independently screened to assess their 
relevance to the review objectives according to the predefined eligibility criteria. 
Full-Text Assessment: Potentially eligible articles were retrieved and evaluated in full text to 
confirm their eligibility for inclusion. 
Any discrepancies encountered during the selection process were resolved through discussion 
and consensus. 
A total of 855 records were identified through database searching. After removal of duplicates 
(n = 183), 672 records were screened based on titles and abstracts. At this stage, 373 records 
were excluded. Subsequently, 299 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 90 
studies were excluded for the following reasons: not meeting inclusion criteria related to 
population, outcomes, or study design. Finally, 209 studies were included in the qualitative 
synthesis.

Data Extraction 
Data extraction was performed using a standardized extraction form developed specifically for 
this review. The following information was collected from each eligible study: the author(s) 
and year of publication, country of study, study design, population characteristics, sample size, 
anthropometric parameters investigated, body composition indicators assessed, measurement 
techniques used, reference values and cut-off points, main outcomes related to physical 
performance, nutrition, metabolic health, and health assessment, as well as the principal 
conclusions reported by the authors.Methodological Quality Assessment and Risk of Bias 
The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies were evaluated using the 
Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tools, according to the study design. 
Data synthesis 
Data synthesis was conducted using a narrative systematic approach due to the substantial 
heterogeneity observed across the included studies in terms of study design, population 
characteristics, anthropometric indicators, outcome measures, and reporting methods, which 
precluded the performance of a quantitative meta-analysis. The selected studies were classified 
into several thematic categories, including traditional anthropometric indicators (body mass 
index, waist circumference, waist-to-hip ratio, and waist-to-height ratio), novel adiposity and 
cardiometabolic risk indices (body adiposity index, body roundness index, a body shape index,

percentage, fat-free mass, fat-free mass index, and body mass fat ratio), and advanced

their applications in sports sciences, nutrition, physical performance evaluation, metabolic 
health, and general health assessment. This thematic synthesis facilitated the identification of 
methodological trends, strengths, limitations, and emerging perspectives in contemporary 
anthropometric research. 
Results and Discussion 
General Body Dimensions 
Stature (Height) and Body Mass 
Stature (height) is the vertical distance from the vertex of the head to the ground in an upright standing 
position, and represents a key anthropometric parameter for assessing growth, nutritional status, and 
overall health 17. Stature is measured using a stadiometer under standardized conditions 17. The stature 
can also be estimated by regression models based on alternative anthropometric parameters such as long 
bones, vertebral dimensions, or limb lengths 18. 
Body mass corresponds to the total mass of the individual and integrates fat mass, lean mass, bone mass, 
and body water. In sport, it is interpreted in relation to stature and body composition. This parameter is 
measured using a calibrated digital or beam balance scale. Subjects wear light clothing and no shoes, 
and measurements are typically recorded to the nearest 0.1 kg under standardized conditions 1. 
Arm Span 
Arm span is the linear distance between the tips of the middle fingers when both arms are fully extended 
horizontally at shoulder level. It is a key anthropometric parameter widely used as a reliable proxy for 
stature, particularly in situations where direct height measurement is difficult or impossible 19. Arm 
span is a stable body dimension, that have a strong positive correlation with height across different 
populations, making it highly valuable in clinical, nutritional studies 20. 
Arm span is measured using a flexible or rigid measuring tape under standardized conditions. 
The subject stands upright with the back against a wall, arms extended laterally at shoulder 
height (90° abduction), elbows fully extended, and palms facing forward (Cherif et al., 2022). 
The measurement is taken from the tip of the middle finger of one hand to that of the opposite

Body Circumferences (Girths) 
Waist Circumference (WC) 
Waist Circumference (WC) is the horizontal measurement of abdominal girth at a standardized 
anatomical level. It reflects central (visceral) adiposity, which is strongly associated with 
cardiometabolic risks such as type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and other metabolic syndromes. Also, WC 
provides a more accurate assessment of fat distribution, particularly abdominal fat accumulation 21,22.  
The measurement of WC must follow a standardized protocol to ensure reliability. The subject stands 
upright with feet together, arms relaxed, and abdomen relaxed. A flexible, non-elastic measuring tape 
is placed horizontally around the abdomen at the midpoint between the lowest rib and the iliac crest , at 
the end of a normal expiration 23 or at the iliac crest level depending on the protocol. The tape should 
be snug but not compress the skin, and the measurement is taken at the end of a normal expiration. The 
value is recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm. Proper training of the assessor and consistency in anatomical 
landmarks are essential to minimize measurement variability 24,25. Table 2 shows the Waist

Hip Circumference (HC) 
This anthropometric parameter is defined as the maximum circumference measured around the buttocks 
at the level of the greatest gluteal protuberance. It reflects peripheral fat distribution and gluteofemoral 
muscle mass and is widely used in the assessment of body composition and cardiometabolic risk, 
particularly when combined with other indices such as the waist-to-hip ratio 27,28.

Overall, anthropometric assessment begins with fundamental body dimensions such as stature, body 
mass, and arm span, which collectively form the basis for morphological and functional evaluation in 
sports science and health research 1,17. 
Stature remains one of the most widely used anthropometric variables due to its strong association with 
genetic growth potential, skeletal development, and sport-specific performance advantages. It is 
particularly relevant in sports where leverage, reach, and biomechanical efficiency play a determining 
role, such as basketball and volleyball. However, its interpretation must consider biological variability 
and environmental influences, including nutrition and developmental conditions 35 
Body mass, when interpreted in isolation, provides limited information regarding body composition, as 
it integrates fat mass, lean mass, bone, and water compartments. Therefore, its relevance increases when 
combined with stature and body composition indicators to assess relative body size and performance 
potential1. 
Arm span has emerged as a reliable surrogate of stature, particularly in populations where direct height 
measurement is not feasible. Its strong correlation with height and relative stability across aging

These primary anthropometric dimensions represent the structural foundation for all derived indices. 
Nevertheless, their isolated use is insufficient for accurate physiological interpretation, which justifies 
the development of composite anthropometric indices. 
Upper Arm Circumference (Relaxed and Flexed) 
According to the International Society for the Advancement of Kinanthropometry (ISAK) standards, 
upper arm circumference is measured at the midpoint between the acromion and olecranon processes, 
previously identified and marked with the arm relaxed. In the relaxed condition (RUAC), the subject 
stands in the anatomical position with the arm hanging freely at the side, muscles fully relaxed. A non-
elastic anthropometric tape is positioned perpendicular to the long axis of the arm at the marked 
midpoint, ensuring it is snug without compressing the soft tissues and remains horizontal. The 
measurement is recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm, typically in duplicate or triplicate, with the mean or 
median retained. This measure reflects total upper arm girth, integrating muscle, subcutaneous fat, and 
bone, and is widely used in sports science to monitor body composition and nutritional status 36–38. 
In the flexed and tensed condition (FUAC), the subject raises the arm and flexes the elbow to 
approximately 90°, performing a maximal voluntary contraction of the biceps. The circumference is 
measured at the same anatomical level or at the point of maximal girth if slightly displaced during 
contraction, following standardized protocols.  
The difference between flexed and relaxed values (ΔAC = FUAC − RUAC) is often used in sports 
science as an indicator of muscle development and contractile capacity 39,40. Furthermore, when 
combined with triceps skinfold thickness (TSF), MUAC allows estimation of muscle and fat 
compartments using equations such as AMC = MUAC − (π × TSF) and UAMA = [MUAC − (π × TSF)]² 
/ (4π), which are widely applied to assess hypertrophy and training adaptations in athletic populations

Thigh Circumference (TC) 
Thigh circumference (TC) is commonly used to assess lower-limb muscle mass, fat distribution, and 
functional symmetry. This parameter is particularly valuable in clinical and sports settings for 
evaluating quadriceps muscle development, sarcopenia, rehabilitation progress, and musculoskeletal 
function 28,44 . In sports science, TC is also applied to monitor training adaptations and detect limb 
asymmetries, especially after injury or during recovery processes 45 
Thigh circumference is measured according to standardized anthropometric procedures. The subject 
stands upright with body weight evenly distributed and thigh muscles relaxed. A non-elastic flexible 
measuring tape is placed horizontally around the thigh, perpendicular to its longitudinal axis, ensuring 
that the tape fits snugly without compressing the soft tissues. Measurement sites may vary depending 
on the purpose of the assessment. In general anthropometry, the circumference is often measured at the 
mid-thigh, located halfway between the inguinal crease and the proximal border of the patella. In 
clinical and rehabilitation settings, a fixed-distance method, commonly 10 cm above the patella, is 
frequently preferred because of its high reproducibility 45,46. To improve reliability, measurements are 
generally performed two or three times, and the mean value is recorded. The Thigh Circumference

 Calf Circumference 
Calf circumference (CC) is defined as the maximum girth of the lower leg, commonly used as a 
surrogate marker of skeletal muscle mass. It is particularly valuable in clinical and epidemiological 
settings for the assessment of sarcopenia, malnutrition, and functional decline, especially in older adults. 
Evidence shows that CC correlates positively with skeletal muscle mass, physical performance, and 
overall health outcomes, including morbidity and mortality risk 47–49. Due to its simplicity, low cost, 
and non-invasive nature, CC is widely recommended when advanced body composition techniques such 
as DXA or BIA are not accessible 28,50. 
The measurement of calf circumference is conducted by a flexible, non-stretchable measuring tape. The 
subject should be either seated with the knee at approximately 90° or standing with weight evenly 
distributed and muscles relaxed. The tape is placed horizontally around the calf at the point of maximum 
circumference, which is identified visually or by moving the tape along the calf to locate the largest 
girth. The measurement should be recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm without compressing the skin. It is 
generally recommended to measure the non-dominant leg and to take at least two readings to ensure 
reliability 47,49,51. The Table 6 presents Calf Circumference Cut-off Values.

 Bone Breadths 
Biacromial Breadth 
Biacromial breadth (BAB), also referred to as shoulder breadth, is a transverse anthropometric 
parameter defined as the maximum horizontal distance between the two acromial processes of the 
scapula. It represents the width of the shoulders and is widely used as an indicator of body frame size, 
upper body morphology, and physical performance potential. This parameter has been shown to 
correlate with stature, muscular development, and athletic performance in various populations, 
particularly in sports sciences and anthropometric profiling studies52–54. Additionally, biacromial

Bi-iliac Breadth 
Bi-iliac breadth (BIB), also known as maximum pelvic breadth, is an important transverse 
anthropometric parameter defined as the maximum horizontal distance between the most lateral points 
of the iliac crests of the pelvis. It reflects pelvic width and is widely used in anthropometry, forensic 
science, and sports sciences to assess body proportions, sexual dimorphism, and body mass

frequently included in predictive models of body mass and morphological variation 61,62. Moreover, bi-
iliac breadth is considered a key skeletal dimension in evaluating pelvic structure and has been linked 
to physiological and functional outcomes such as thermoregulation and locomotor efficiency 63,64.

(horizontal) plane. In some protocols, the caliper may be slightly angled (approximately 45°) to 
accurately capture the most lateral points of the pelvis. The measurement is recorded to the nearest 0.1 
cm without compressing the soft tissues, ensuring accuracy, reliability, and reproducibility 65,66.

and is widely recognized as a major indicator of body composition, nutritional status, metabolic health, 
obesity risk, and physical performance 134. Unlike Body Mass Index (BMI), FM% differentiates fat 
mass from fat-free mass (FFM), including muscles, bones, organs, and body water, thereby providing 
a more comprehensive assessment of body composition and health status 134. In sports sciences and 
clinical nutrition, FM% is extensively used to monitor obesity, athletic performance, and

physical performance, whereas excessive adiposity is strongly linked to cardiovascular and metabolic

Fat-free mass (FFM) includes all non-fat components of the body, such as skeletal muscle, bone, organs, 
and body water, and is a key determinant of strength and power performance 153. It is commonly 
calculated as total body mass minus fat mass, or more specifically as body mass minus the sum of fat 
mass and bone mass 154. FFM can also be assessed using advanced techniques such as fan-beam dual-
energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), which provides accurate measurements of total and regional lean 
mass, including skeletal muscle mass 155. 
To account for differences in body size, the fat-free mass index (FFMI) is used as a height-adjusted 
indicator, calculated as FFM (kg) divided by height squared (m²). FFMI allows comparison across 
athletes by normalizing FFM relative to stature and is useful for classifying low, moderate, or high FFM 
levels in relation to performance and injury risk 156. More recently, bioelectrical impedance-based 
predictive models have been developed using four-compartment reference models, showing good 
validity for estimating FFM in athletes 157. However, despite variations in muscularity and 
musculoskeletal development, studies indicate that these factors do not significantly alter the density of 
FFM, supporting the general applicability of standard assumptions in body composition models 158. 
Regarding reference values, FFMI in healthy adult populations typically ranges from 16-19 kg/m² in 
women and 18-22 kg/m² in men, while trained athletes present higher values depending on sport 
discipline. Resistance-trained male athletes often show FFMI values around 21–25 kg/m², with elite 
strength or power athletes occasionally exceeding 25 kg/m², whereas endurance athletes generally 
present lower values 156. These values provide useful normative ranges for evaluating muscular 
development, performance potential, and training adaptation across populations.

and body water 161. By normalizing lean mass to stature, FFMI allows valid comparisons across 
individuals with different anthropometric characteristics and sport disciplines 153,162. It is widely used 
in sports science to characterize physique profiles, monitor training adaptations, and evaluate nutritional 
status, especially in strength, endurance, and mixed-sport athletes 159,160.

Body Muscle-to-Fat Ratio (BMFR) 
The Body Muscle-to-Fat Ratio (BMFR) is a recent anthropometric and body composition index used to 
evaluate the balance between skeletal muscle mass and fat mass. It is used as a marker for assessing 
insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, and cardiometabolic health 169. BMFR reflects the physiological 
interaction between metabolically active muscle tissue and adipose tissue, where higher muscle mass 
contributes to improved glucose utilization and insulin sensitivity, whereas excessive fat accumulation 
is associated with chronic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction. BMFR is therefore increasingly 
recognized as a useful indicator in obesity research, sports sciences, and clinical nutrition 170.

composition assessment techniques such as dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA). It is calculated

Somatotype (Heath-Carter Method) 
The Heath–Carter somatotype is a standardized anthropometric method used to quantitatively describe 
human body shape through three independent components: endomorphy, mesomorphy, and ectomorphy 
171,172. It is considered the most widely used somatotyping method in sports sciences, nutrition, and body 
composition assessment 82. The method evaluates the relative contribution of adiposity, musculoskeletal 
development, and body linearity to overall physique 173. 
In the Heath–Carter system, endomorphy reflects relative fatness, mesomorphy represents 
musculoskeletal robustness, and ectomorphy indicates body linearity and slenderness 174. Somatotype 
is expressed as a three-number rating such as 2–5–3, corresponding respectively to endomorphy, 

============================================================
PAGE 36

mesomorphy, and ectomorphy 172. This method is widely applied in talent identification, sports 
performance evaluation, nutritional assessment, and health-related body composition studies 175,176. 
The Heath–Carter somatotype method is based on 10 standardized anthropometric measurements 
performed according to the ISAK (International Society for the Advancement of Kinanthropometry) 
guidelines 177. These measurements include body height, body mass, four skinfolds (triceps, 
subscapular, suprailiac, and medial calf), two bone breadths (humerus and femur), and two limb 
circumferences (flexed arm and calf girths) 171,178.  
The endomorphy component is estimated from the sum of skinfold thicknesses corrected for height 172. 
 Mesomorphy is calculated using bone breadths, corrected limb girths, and stature measurements 173.  
Ectomorphy is determined from the Height–Weight Ratio (HWR), which reflects body linearity 174.

Phantom Z-Scores (Ross Phantom Model) 
The Phantom Z-score model (Ross Phantom Model) is a standardized anthropometric reference system 
utilized in the evaluation of body proportionality by expressing an individual’s anthropometric 
measurements as deviations from a theoretical “phantom” human model (Ross & Wilson concept)182. 
This phantom represents a sex-independent reference body with fixed mean values for height, mass, 
skinfolds, circumferences, and bone breadths 182,183. 
Phantom Z-scores indicate how many standard deviations an individual’s measurement is above or 
below the reference population. A Z-score of 0 means the individual matches the phantom standard, 
while positive or negative values indicate greater or lower proportionality for a given anthropometric 
trait 32,184. This method is widely used in sports science to compare athletes independently of age, sex, 
and body size 185,186 
The Phantom Z-score method is based on standard anthropometric measurements including skinfold 
thicknesses, body circumferences, bone breadths, body mass, and stature, obtained using ISAK 
protocols 32,183.

Discussion  
Despite their widespread use in sports science, nutrition, and public health, anthropometric 
parameters and body composition indices present several methodological and practical 
limitations that should be considered when interpreting results. Although these measurements 
are inexpensive, non-invasive, and easy to perform, their predictive accuracy varies according 
to age, sex, ethnicity, training status, and physiological characteristics of the population under 
investigation 199; 200). 
Body mass index (BMI) remains one of the most commonly used anthropometric indicators 
because of its simplicity and applicability in large-scale epidemiological studies. However, 
BMI does not distinguish fat mass from lean body mass and may therefore misclassify highly 
trained athletes as overweight or obese due to increased muscularity rather than excessive 
adiposity (199. Furthermore, BMI provides no information regarding fat distribution, a key 
determinant of cardiometabolic risk 201. 
Circumference-based measurements including waist circumference (WC), waist-to-hip ratio 
(WHR), and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) offer additional information regarding central 
adiposity and cardiometabolic health. Nevertheless, these measurements remain sensitive to 
anatomical landmark identification, measurement technique, breathing phase, and postural 

============================================================
PAGE 41
============================================================
39 
 
variations, which may affect reliability and reproducibility 200,202. Moreover, cut-off values 
often differ among ethnic groups and populations, limiting the universal applicability of 
currently available reference standards 203. 
Additionally, the accuracy of Skinfold thickness is strongly dependent on evaluator expertise, 
caliper quality, and the prediction equations employed (Lewandowski et al., 2022; 200). 
Prediction equations developed in one population may not be transferable to other ethnic 
groups, age categories, or athletic disciplines, potentially leading to systematic estimation 
errors 202. Also, skinfold measurements become increasingly challenging in individuals with 
severe obesity and may underestimate visceral adiposity, which has important clinical 
implications 201.  
As regarding to breadth measurements, such as humeral and femoral breadths, provide useful 
information regarding skeletal frame size and somatotype classification. Nevertheless, these 
measurements require strict standardization and experienced evaluators to ensure reliability. 
Small errors in landmark identification may significantly affect skeletal proportionality indices 
and subsequent body composition estimations 200. 
Similarly, body composition indices such as the Fat-Free Mass Index (FFMI), Body Roundness 
Index (BRI), Conicity Index (CI), A Body Shape Index (ABSI), and Visceral Adiposity Index 
(VAI) have been developed to overcome some of the limitations of traditional anthropometric 
measures. While these indices often demonstrate stronger associations with cardiometabolic 
risk factors than BMI alone, their interpretation remains challenging because population-
specific reference values are still evolving and consensus regarding optimal cut-off points has 
not yet been fully established 199,201. 
Another important limitation of anthropometric assessment is its inability to directly quantify 
tissue composition. Anthropometric measurements provide indirect estimations of body 
compartments and may not accurately reflect regional fat distribution, intramuscular adipose 
tissue accumulation, hydration status, or muscle quality 204,205. Consequently, discrepancies 
may arise when anthropometric estimates are compared with reference techniques such as dual-
energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or advanced 
bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) methods 204. 
Future developments in anthropometric science are expected to focus on the integration of 
digital technologies, artificial intelligence, and machine learning algorithms into body 
composition assessment 206. Advances in three-dimensional body scanning, computer vision 
systems, and wearable technologies may facilitate rapid, non-invasive, and highly standardized 

============================================================
PAGE 42
============================================================
40 
 
measurements in both clinical and sports settings 207,208. Artificial intelligence-based image 
analysis has the potential to improve body composition prediction by combining 
anthropometric data with movement patterns, physiological markers, and performance metrics 
206. 
Furthermore, future research should prioritize the development of population-specific 
predictive models that account for age, sex, ethnicity, biological maturation, and athletic 
specialization 199,203. Such approaches may improve the accuracy and applicability of 
anthropometric assessments while supporting personalized nutrition, precision medicine, and 
individualized training interventions. The integration of anthropometric, physiological, 
biochemical, and omics-derived data is likely to represent the next stage in body composition 
research, providing more comprehensive tools for health monitoring, injury prevention, and 
performance optimization in both athletic and clinical populations 201. 
Future Perspectives 
Future developments in anthropometric assessment are expected to focus on improving 
measurement accuracy, standardization, and personalization through the integration of 
emerging technologies and advanced analytical approaches. Although traditional 
anthropometric methods remain widely used because of their simplicity, low cost, and field 
applicability, they are inherently limited by measurement error, operator dependency, 
population-specific variability, and their inability to directly assess tissue composition 
199,200,202. 
One of the most promising directions involves the integration of digital technologies into body 
composition assessment. Recent advances in wearable devices, digital monitoring systems, and 
portable assessment tools are creating new opportunities for continuous and real-time 
evaluation of morphological and physiological characteristics in both athletic and clinical 
populations 207,208. Such technologies may facilitate longitudinal monitoring of body 
composition changes while reducing some of the limitations associated with conventional 
anthropometric measurements. 
Artificial intelligence, machine learning, and computer vision technologies are also expected 
to play an increasingly important role in sports science and body composition research. These 
approaches have demonstrated considerable potential for processing large datasets, identifying 
complex relationships among anthropometric variables, and improving predictive modeling of 
health and performance outcomes 206. The integration of artificial intelligence with 

============================================================
PAGE 43
============================================================

anthropometric assessment may contribute to more objective, automated, and individualized 
evaluations while minimizing observer-related variability. 
Future research should also prioritize the development of population-specific prediction 
models. Current anthropometric equations and reference standards are frequently derived from 
specific populations and may not be fully applicable across different ethnicities, age groups, 
sexes, or athletic disciplines 199,209. Consequently, the establishment of updated normative 
databases and population-specific predictive algorithms will be essential to improve the 
validity and practical applicability of anthropometric assessments. 
In sports science, increasing attention is being directed toward multimodal approaches that 
combine anthropometric measurements with bioelectrical impedance analysis, physiological 
markers, training-load data, and performance indicators 200. Such integrated models may 
provide a more comprehensive understanding of the interactions between body composition, 
physical performance, recovery, and health status than anthropometric measures alone. 
Another important challenge concerns the validation and standardization of emerging body 
composition indices. Although indices such as ABSI, BRI, CI, and VAI have shown promising 
associations with metabolic and cardiovascular outcomes, further longitudinal and multicenter 
investigations are required to establish robust reference values and determine their applicability 
in diverse populations, including elite athletes, older adults, and clinical populations 199,201. 
Finally, the future of anthropometric science is likely to move toward integrated and 
personalized assessment frameworks that combine anthropometric, physiological, behavioral, 
and digital health data. The convergence of wearable technologies, artificial intelligence, 
bioelectrical impedance databases, and advanced analytical tools may enable more precise, 
individualized, and dynamic monitoring of body composition, health status, and athletic 
performance across the lifespan 208,209. 
Conclusion 
Anthropometric assessment remains an essential and practical tool for evaluating body 
composition, physical performance, nutritional status, and health-related risks. Traditional 
measurements and advanced indices collectively provide valuable information for athlete 
profiling, metabolic risk evaluation, and clinical monitoring. However, the limitations of 
certain indicators, particularly BMI, highlight the need for a multidimensional approach 
integrating multiple anthropometric parameters with advanced body composition techniques. 
Future research should focus on improving standardization, developing population-specific
